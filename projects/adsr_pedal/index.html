ADSR_pedal

Motivation

I like playing the electric bass. Something that I've I've always found cool are effect pedals, which basically allow you to change the sound of your instrument at the press of a button. It hit me that these are typically just analog circuits that filter your guitar signal is a cool way. In theory, I've picked up enough knowledge from my Analog Circuits classes to figure out how these things work and design one myself.

Goals

So I want to create a pedal. My first thought is to determine what type of sound I want to produce. Of course the basic filters (e.g. passive RLC) would be simple to achieve, only requiring 2-3 components. However, these wouldn't necessarily change the timbre/mood of the sound, merely changing the volume (and phase) depending on the pitch of the note.

Thus, it seems like a cooler option would be to use active circuitry instead. This isn't a baseless thought to have, either. This pedal that I have been eyeballing uses a handful of active components to make a really cool sound. Another inspiration of mine is the synthesizer Youtuber Moritz Klein, who has great videos going through the development of keyboard synthesizer mods. In particular, he has a great video going through the creation of a similar filter.

So, what I want to do is create that filter that operates upon the knowledge I have picked up from my analog circuits courses.

Development

Research

The core of the pedal will be the one presented in Moritx's Youtube video. However, there is an immediate issue with pulling it directly. The one that he uses expects a digital input that swings from 0-9V DC, which the filter shapes into a cool waveform each oscillation. Upon further research, the raw output from an electric bass is not digital and only peaks at a voltage of around 100-300mV. As such, there needs some to be some pre-processing before the input reaches the ADSR filter.

We'll use a 9V DC power suppy to power our pedal, so we'll bias the signal around 4.5V to maximize our operating voltage. We do this by adding our input signal with a 4.5V source into a unity gain buffer. Our small signal still only operate with a 300mV amplitude. We will amplify it using a non-inverting amplifier scheme. We can use a simple opamp comparator to turn it into a digital signal. We'll compare it to the output of a potentiometer so that the can control the threshold voltage to our liking.

This new digital signal can be run through Klein's ADSR circuit. One final modification we need to do is to adjust the output voltage. Klein's filter will produce a signal that swings from 0-9V and we want to produce a signal that is similar in scale to the input signal. To achieve this, we use a potentiometer and voltage divider to scale down the signal to a reasonable level.

Simulation

Before I commit to this design, it would be wise to determine if this is a viable design. I will be using LTspice as my software of choice to simulate this circuit. I start by implementing a power supply filter, which stabilizes the 9V signal and provides a 4.5V net as well. As our stand-in for the input bass signal, a 300mV-amplitude sinusoid will be used and multiple frequencies from 40-400Hz will be tested (representing the range of the bass).

With potentiometers set to their median ("default") values, the filter is successful for frequencies between 40-100Hz. This is a good sign, as this represents the range of the lower three strings of the bass. However, we see that the output is clipped for the 400Hz simulation as a result of a low-pass RC filter within the circuit. However, by adjusting the potentiometer in this filter we see successful results, though it does alter the shape of the waveform.

The simulations also indicate that the pedal will have a nominal current draw of around 11.5mA, which is acceptable.

PCB Design

PCB design was done in KiCad 9.0. A key feature that I wanted to implement was a modular pedal design, where I could design a singular face plate where different filters can be swapped out. A design choice that I have made was to integrate only the potentiometers, LED indicators, and power/signal interfaces onto the face plate. This was with the intention that the face plate circuitry be as simple as possible. This leaves the power and ADSR filter as a separate breakout board. However, in future iterations, I would actually prefer to move the power filtering onto the face plate itself, as the current configuration requires that all future filter modules to include power filtering as well.

In an effort to reduce costs, I wanted to combine both the face and filter in the same PCB layout. I was aware of panelization as a method to fabricate multiple boards at scale. However, because I was working at such a small scale (5 units), full panelization would not be necessary. Instead I borrowed a technique and implemented "mouse bites" between the two boards so that they could be fabricated as one, rather than as two separate projects.

Initial Product

I quickly 3D-modelled a wall structure for the front panel and other jacks to mount to. Once all of the components were soldered to the face plate, the pedal strongly matched with my intended vision for the final product. Let's talk about the pros and cons of the pedal.

Pros

  • The pedal looks really cool

Cons

  • The pedal does not work at all
When I switch to the pedal, the output is pure silence. This holds regardless of how hard I play the bass or what values I set the dials to. My first instinct is that something is shorting the output signal to ground. However, investigations into the filter and face plate indicate that this is not the issue. Perhaps it is an issue with the connections on the face plate, but yet again it's components are behaving without issue. Unfortunately, this means that the issue likely lies with the filter itself.

Troubleshooting

The issue is likely as such: at some point in the filter, the signal is saturating to one of the rail voltages of an op amp. As such, the resultant signal is a pure DC signal and there is no AC signal at the output of the pedal. Because many op amps are used in the filter, locating where this failure is occuring can be difficult. I am opting to recreate the filter on a bread board, progressively moving through the circuit and altering sections as needed.

An interesting conundrum that I have is that I am limited to a multimeter as my sole tool for measurement. Luckily, because I am only working in the audible frequency range I am able to just use my ears to determine how I am affecting my signal.

Power filter

RC filter resistor was causing clicking when switching to the pedal. No background hum was audible during testing and was deemed redundant. This component was removed.

Input buffer

Input buffer circuit was largely successful, though specific values for the bias resistor and buffer capacitor were changed for component availability.

Signal amplifier

This is likely where the main issue resided. My initial assumption was that the signal coming from the bass would have a peak of 100-300mV. As such, I would be able to amplify it by around 10 times before I would see saturation from the op amp output. However, in testing I could only amplify it by 1.5 times before the signal reached the rail voltage. Thus, I suspect that the leading cause of failure is the bass providing a signal with a greater amplitude that anticipated, or I have a fundamental misuderstanding of the functional limits of an op amp (both are possible, but I am limited by the tools at hand do determine which is true).

To correct for this, I am adding a trimpot so that I can adjust the gain to its maximum possible value before clipping occurs. Moreover, I am adding a bias resistor to limit the current entering the op amp and reducing the buffer capacitance so I can use a film capacitor instead.
